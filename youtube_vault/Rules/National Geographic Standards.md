# National Geographic Standards

> [!important] Real science, real wonder
> The show's promise to parents and teachers is that the science is **true**. Educational value is **mandatory** — but it must arrive as a "wow," never a lecture.

## The four standards
> [!check] Every fact must be:
> 1. **Real** — an actual, documented behavior or phenomenon.
> 2. **Verified** — checked against a credible source *before* it goes in the script.
> 3. **A "wow" moment** — surprising enough to make a kid (and an adult) go *"whoa — really?"*
> 4. **Educationally valuable** — it teaches something true about the natural world.

## How facts are used
- **One concept per episode.** Depth over breadth — fully land a single idea (see [[Storytelling Rules]]).
- **Show, don't state.** Dramatize the behavior through the guest creature's problem; let **Theodore** (the narrator) frame it.
- **Accuracy in the visuals too.** Creature anatomy and behavior must be plausible — see [[Bentley Master Prop]] and [[Pip the Hermit Crab]].

## Verification workflow
1. Draft the fact in the script.
2. **Verify** against a credible source (marine biology / natural-history references); note the source.
3. Sanity-check the **"wow"** — would it surprise a curious 7-year-old?
4. Confirm it can be **dramatized** through the guest's story.
5. It must pass the **Fact QA** gate in the [[Episode QA Checklist]] before lock.

## Example — Episode 1
The **vacancy chain**: when a hermit crab outgrows its shell, a *whole line* of crabs trades up at once — one empty shell rehomes the entire tide pool. True, documented, and a genuine "wow." See [[Episode 1 - The Voice in the Shell]].
